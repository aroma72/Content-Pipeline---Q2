### Scene 3: Testing vs Evaluation (2:00-5:00)

**Visual**: Animated diagram showing the difference

**Script**:
"Let's be crystal clear about the difference.

Testing asks: Does the code run? Does it break? If I give it input X, do I get output Y?

Testing is important. You definitely need it. But it's narrow. It's focused on the code.

Evaluation asks a different question: Does the agent achieve its goal? Does the output actually help users? Is it high quality?

Think of it this way.

[Visual: Factory quality control analogy]

Testing is like checking: Does the machine work? Does it turn on? Does it stop when I turn it off?

Evaluation is like asking: Does the product that comes out of the machine actually work for customers? Can they use it? Do they like it?

Both matter. But they're measuring different things."

**Visual**: Show comparison table:

|--------|---------|-----------|
| Asks | Does code work? | Does agent achieve goals? |
| Measures | Code execution | Output quality |
| Scope | Individual functions | End-to-end behavior |
| When | During development | Continuous |

**Script**:

### Scene 6: The Framework (10:00-12:00)

**Visual**: Animated diagram of the MEASURE loop

**Script**:
"Evaluation isn't a one-time thing. It's a continuous loop.

We call it the MEASURE loop.

[Visual: Animate each step]

**M** — Measure what matters. Pick 3-5 metrics that tell you if your agent is working.

**E** — Explore failures. When something goes wrong, collect the data and understand what happened.

**A** — Assess root causes. Why did it fail? Was it the prompt? The data? The tool?

**S** — Sum up health. Compile your findings. 'Agent pass rate is 92%.'

**U** — Unblock improvements. Based on what you learned, what do you fix?

And then you loop back to M. Measure again. See if you improved.

This loop runs forever. Measure, improve, measure, improve.

That's how you build and maintain high-quality agents."

### Scene 2: MEASURE Step 1 - Measure (0:45-3:00)

**Visual**: Screen share showing a blank evaluation plan

**Script**:
"First step: **Measure**. Pick what matters.

This is where a lot of people get lost. They want to measure everything. Speed, accuracy, cost, latency, clarity, completeness...

Stop. You can't optimize for everything.

Here's the rule: Pick 3 to 5 metrics. No more.

Why? Because if you track 20 metrics, you can't act on them. You get analysis paralysis.

[Visual: Show a list of 20 possible metrics]

But 3-5? That's manageable. You can actually act on those.

So how do you choose?

Ask yourself: What is this agent supposed to do? What would it look like if it succeeded?

[Visual: Example - Learning Content Generator]

Let's say your agent generates learning materials. It should:
1. Cover the key concepts (completeness)
2. Be accurate (not make up facts)
3. Be clear (learners can understand it)
4. Be published on time (speed matters)

So your metrics might be:
1. **Completeness**: % of concepts covered from the source material
2. **Accuracy**: % of facts that are correct (verified by human review)
3. **Clarity**: Learner rating of understandability (1-5 scale)
4. **Timeliness**: Avg hours from recording to publication

That's four metrics. Manageable. Actionable.

Notice: These metrics come from the goals. The goals come first. The metrics are just how you measure progress toward those goals."

**Visual**: Show the process:

(Concept coverage → Completeness metric)
(Correctness → Accuracy metric)
(Understandability → Clarity metric)
(Speed → Timeliness metric)

### Scene 5: Three Types of Evaluation (7:30-11:00)

**Visual**: Three columns on screen: Metrics | Automated Checks | Human Eval

**Script**:
"Now, here's the question: How do you actually measure?

There are three ways. You should use all three.

**Type 1: Metrics** (Quantitative)

Metrics are numbers. Accuracy, latency, cost, pass rate.

They're calculated from data.

[Visual: Show metric calculation code (pseudocode)]

def calculate_pass_rate(results):
    passed = count(results where result.status == 'pass')
    total = count(results)
    return passed / total

It's simple. You gather data. You calculate. You get a number.

The advantage: You can track it over time. See trends. Spot regressions fast.

The disadvantage: Metrics can lie. A metric can be 95%, but you're still wrong about something.

So you need the other types.

**Type 2: Automated Checks** (Binary)

These are pass/fail checks.

[Visual: Show example checks]

- Does the glossary have at least 5 terms? Yes/No
- Is the document longer than 100 words? Yes/No
- Does the output contain the required sections? Yes/No

These run on every output. Fast. Cheap. Reliable.

The advantage: They're simple. They catch structural problems. They're gates. If a check fails, don't publish.

The disadvantage: They're binary. They don't measure quality. A glossary can have 5 terms and still be bad.

**Type 3: Human Evaluation** (Qualitative)

An expert reads the output and judges it.

[Visual: Show a rubric]

Is the explanation clear? Rate 1-5.
Is the content accurate? Rate 1-5.
Is the content complete? Rate 1-5.

It's subjective. It's slow. It's expensive. But it catches what metrics and checks miss.

The advantage: Nuance. Context. You understand *why* something is good or bad.

The disadvantage: It's slow. You can't do it on every output. You do it on samples.

**The Hybrid Approach**

You use all three together.

[Visual: Flowchart]

1. Run automated checks on every output. Fast gate.
2. Calculate metrics daily. Track trends.
3. Spot-check with human eval weekly. Validate metrics against reality.

If metrics and human eval disagree, investigate. Maybe your metric is wrong. Maybe your human judgment is off.

This is how you build trust in your evaluation system."

**Visual**: Show the three types working together in a diagram

### Scene 2: The Metric Design Process (0:45-3:30)

**Visual**: Whiteboard animation showing the process

**Script**:
"Here's the framework for designing metrics.

**Step 1: Define Success**

What does it look like when this agent is working well?

Not technically. Not 'the code compiles.'

Practically. In the real world.

[Visual: Examples for different agents]

If it's a support chatbot:
- Users get their problems solved
- They're satisfied with the interaction
- Resolution time is reasonable

If it's a content generator:
- Content covers the key topics
- Content is accurate
- Learners understand it
- It's published on time

You need to articulate this clearly.

**Step 2: Translate to Measurable Criteria**

Now translate those fuzzy goals into measurable things.

'Users are satisfied' → Can't measure directly. But you can ask them. Rate satisfaction 1-5.

'Content is accurate' → Can't measure directly. But you can have an expert verify. % of facts that are correct.

'Learners understand it' → Can't measure directly. But you can ask learners. 'Rate clarity 1-5.'

'Published on time' → Can measure. Hours from input to publication.

**Step 3: Design the Metric**

Now you design how to measure each criterion.

[Visual: Code example]

def calculate_accuracy_metric(outputs, ground_truth):
    correct = 0
    for output, truth in zip(outputs, ground_truth):
        if output == truth:
            correct += 1
    accuracy = correct / len(outputs)
    return accuracy

It's simple. Gather data. Calculate. Return a number.

**Step 4: Set a Target**

What's good? What's bad?

You need a baseline.

[Visual: Show target setting]

Your agent starts at 70% accuracy. That's the baseline.

Your target is 85%. That's where you want to be.

Anything below 70% is a regression. Anything above 85% is great.

These targets guide your work. You're not chasing abstract perfection. You're chasing specific, achievable goals.

**Step 5: Track Over Time**

Now measure regularly. Daily. Weekly.

Plot the trend. Do you see progress?

[Visual: Line graph showing accuracy trending up]

This is where you catch problems early. If accuracy drops from 85% to 78%, something broke. Investigate."

### Scene 3: Common Mistakes (3:30-6:00)

**Visual**: Screen showing common pitfalls

**Script**:
"Here are the mistakes people make when designing metrics.

**Mistake 1: Metric Gaming**

You optimize for the metric, not the actual goal.

[Visual: Example]

You set a metric: 'Agent response time. Goal: < 1 second.'

Now your agent gives wrong answers, but really fast.

Response time metric: 0.8 seconds. ✓ Passes!

But the answers are garbage.

You gamed the metric.

To avoid this: Always validate metrics against human judgment. If metrics are green but humans say 'this is bad,' then your metrics are wrong."

**Visual**: Show metric vs reality
- Metric: 0.8 sec (good)
- Human judgment: Wrong answer (bad)

**Script**:
"**Mistake 2: Incomplete Coverage**

Your metric only measures part of the problem.

[Visual: Example]

You measure accuracy. 92% of facts are correct.

But you don't measure completeness. 30% of required information is missing.

Learners read the content. It's accurate, but incomplete. They don't learn everything.

Your metric said success. Reality said failure.

To avoid this: Measure multiple dimensions. Not just accuracy. Also completeness. Also clarity. Also timeliness.

**Mistake 3: No Baseline**

You measure something. You get 75%. Is that good?

You don't know. You have no baseline.

Was it 50% last week? 75% is progress.
Or was it 90% last week? 75% is a regression.

Without a baseline, metrics are meaningless.

To avoid this: Always track historical data. Know your baseline. Know your target."

**Visual**: Show baseline importance
Week 1: 75% (is this good or bad? We don't know)
vs
Week 1: 75% (baseline), Week 2: 85% (target), Today: 82% (we're getting close!)

### Scene 4: Code Example (6:00-9:00)

**Visual**: Screen share with code editor

**Script**:
"Let me show you how to implement a metric in Python.

[Visual: Open a Python file]

Here's a simple accuracy metric.

def calculate_accuracy_metric(test_results):
    '''
    Calculate % of test cases that passed.
    '''
    if not test_results:
        return 0
    
    passed = sum(1 for r in test_results if r['passed'])
    total = len(test_results)
    accuracy = (passed / total) * 100
    
    return accuracy

Let me explain:
- We have a list of test results
- Each result has a 'passed' field (True or False)
- We count how many passed
- We divide by total
- We get a percentage

Simple.

Now let's use it:

[Visual: Show usage code]

results = [
    {'passed': True, 'output': '...'},
    {'passed': True, 'output': '...'},
    {'passed': False, 'output': '...'},
    {'passed': True, 'output': '...'},
]

accuracy = calculate_accuracy_metric(results)
print(f'Accuracy: {accuracy}%')

Output: Accuracy: 75.0%

That's it. You have a metric.

Now, what do you do with it?

You track it over time.

[Visual: Show tracking code]

metrics_over_time = []

for day in range(1, 8):  # One week
    daily_results = run_tests(day)
    daily_accuracy = calculate_accuracy_metric(daily_results)
    metrics_over_time.append({
        'day': day,
        'accuracy': daily_accuracy
    })

for metric in metrics_over_time:
    print(f'Day {metric['day']}: {metric['accuracy']}%')

Output:
Day 1: 85.0%
Day 2: 84.0%
Day 3: 86.0%
Day 4: 87.0%
Day 5: 88.0%
Day 6: 89.0%
Day 7: 90.0%

Look at that trend. Accuracy is improving. That's good.

If on Day 2 you saw a drop to 50%, you'd investigate. What broke?"

**Visual**: Show the trend line going up

### Scene 5: Common Pitfalls in Improvement (9:30-11:00)

**Visual**: Screen showing pitfalls

**Script**:
"Here are mistakes people make during the improvement cycle:

**Pitfall 1: Ignoring Metrics That Get Worse**

You improve pass rate, but accuracy drops. You celebrate pass rate. You ignore accuracy.

Don't. Investigate. Understand the tradeoff. Maybe you need to refine your approach.

**Pitfall 2: Changing Too Much at Once**

You rewrite the entire prompt. You change the data. You modify the model. And metrics improve.

Now what? What actually caused the improvement? You don't know.

Instead: Change one thing. Measure. Understand the impact.

**Pitfall 3: Not Measuring Continuously**

You measure once. You see bad metrics. You improve. You measure once more.

That's not continuous improvement. That's random.

Instead: Measure daily. See trends. Spot regressions immediately.

**Pitfall 4: Optimizing for the Wrong Metric**

You optimize for speed. The agent gets faster. But accuracy plummets.

You hit your target. But your users are unhappy.

Instead: Optimize for the goal, not the metric. Metrics are proxies. Make sure they align with what matters."
