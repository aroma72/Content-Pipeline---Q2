# Research: Look beyond the reaction (Iceberg + SCARF)

## Verified facts

1. **SCARF origin.** David Rock, "SCARF: a brain-based model for collaborating with and influencing others", *NeuroLeadership Journal*, Issue 1, 2008, pp. 1-9. Source: original paper https://schoolguide.casel.org/uploads/sites/2/2018/12/SCARF-NeuroleadershipArticle.pdf (2008); confirmed by Mind Tools https://www.mindtools.com/akswgc0/david-rocks-scarf-model/ (accessed 2026-10-07).
2. **Rock's own definitions:** "Status is about relative importance to others. Certainty concerns being able to predict the future. Autonomy provides a sense of control over events. Relatedness is a sense of safety with others, of friend rather than foe. And fairness is a perception of fair exchanges between people." (Rock 2008, same URL)
3. **The core idea:** the brain's organising principle is "minimize threat and maximize reward", an approach/avoid response. Each of the five domains can trigger either one. (Rock 2008, citing Gordon 2000)
4. **Threat costs thinking.** Rock argues that threat activation reduces the resources available to the prefrontal cortex, which hurts working memory. In his words, "When feeling threatened by one's boss, it is harder to find smart answers." (Rock 2008)
5. **Bad weighs more than good.** "Bad events have stronger and more lasting consequences than comparable good events." Baumeister et al., *Review of General Psychology* 5, 323-370 (2001). Source: https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/bad-is-stronger-than-good/ (2001), cited in Rock 2008.
6. **Feedback can be a status threat.** Rock writes that a status threat "can occur through giving advice or instructions". He also says the question "can I offer you some feedback" sets off a status threat in most people. (Rock 2008)
7. **Wording can lower the autonomy threat.** Rock's example: "Here's two options that could work, which would you prefer?" gets a better response than "Here's what you have to do now." (Rock 2008)
8. **Honest caveat on the science.** SCARF "has not been scientifically validated in the strict sense". It is a practical framework, not a proven brain law: https://t2informatik.de/en/smartpedia/scarf-model/ (accessed 2026-10-07). An NHS practice-development paper raises the same "neuromania" concern: https://www.fons.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/IDPJ_12_01_05.pdf (2022). The "social pain looks like physical pain" finding (Eisenberger 2003) is still debated, and replications have questioned it: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11592030/ (2024). **Script rule: say "our brains react *as if*…" and teach SCARF as a lens, not as proven neuroscience.**
9. **Iceberg origin (often misattributed).** Freud never used the iceberg metaphor in his published writings. People link it to him, but it came from others, for example Fechner and G. Stanley Hall. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31633371/ (2019); Quote Investigator: https://quoteinvestigator.com/2024/08/07/mind-iceberg/ (2024). In HR, David McClelland's 1970s competency iceberg is best known. Skills and knowledge sit above the line. Self-image, traits and motives sit below it: https://kennethsmit.com/en/kennisbank/ijsbergmodel-mcclelland-competenties/ (accessed 2026-10-07). **Script rule: don't say "Freud's iceberg". Use "the iceberg model of behaviour".**

## SCARF for a P&C Buddy

| Domain | Plain definition | Typical trigger | Say / do to lower the threat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Status** | "Do I matter here? Am I respected?" | Being corrected in front of others; someone junior being consulted first | Talk in private. Name what they do well first: "You know this team better than anyone. What's your read?" |
| **Certainty** | "Can I predict what happens next?" | Restructure rumours; a vague "we need to talk" | Give what you know, what you don't, and when they'll hear more: "Here's what's decided, here's what isn't, I'll update you Thursday." |
| **Autonomy** | "Do I have any say?" | Being told how to do the work, not just what to deliver | Offer a choice: "Two options could work. Which would you prefer?" (Rock's own line) |
| **Relatedness** | "Are you with me or against me?" | Being new, left out of a group chat or lunch, or a cold first contact | Start with a human moment before business. Introduce them to one person. "You're not on your own with this." |
| **Fairness** | "Are the rules the same for everyone?" | Unequal leave approvals, or an unexplained promotion | Explain the criteria openly: "Here's how the decision was made, and here's how you can raise it." |

## Live-conversation method: "Pause, Probe, Pin, Respond"

1. **Pause.** Treat the reaction as the tip of the iceberg. Don't argue with it.
2. **Probe.** Ask one curious, open question: "What's worrying you most about this?"
3. **Pin it.** Silently ask yourself which SCARF domain fits best (S? C? A? R? F?). Usually one or two dominate.
4. **Respond to that need.** Use the matching move from the table above.
5. **Check.** "Did I understand that right?" This also gives back status and relatedness.

## Misconceptions to pre-empt

- **"The reaction is the problem / they're just difficult."** The reaction is a signal, not a character flaw.
- **"Understanding = excusing."** Knowing why doesn't remove accountability. Address the need, then the behaviour.
- **"SCARF is a label I tell people."** Never say "you're having a status threat." It's a private lens for the Buddy, not a diagnosis to announce. It also isn't a checklist: people differ in which domains matter most to them.

## Analogy

- **Best:** the **iceberg itself**. A ship hits what's below the waterline, not the tip. Pair it with the **smoke alarm** image: the alarm is loud (the reaction), so look for what triggered it (the SCARF need). Both are visual and culturally neutral.
- **Avoid:** "lizard brain / amygdala hijack" (pop-neuroscience, overclaims); "Freud's iceberg" (misattributed); medical or diagnostic language ("triggered", "trauma").

## Three workplace situations (invented, gender-neutral)

**1. The snapped reply.** At the Monday stand-up, the regional lead announces that field visits will now be logged in a new app. A field coordinator says sharply, "Fine, whatever you want."
- *Hidden domain:* **Autonomy** (and Certainty: "Will this be used to monitor me?").
- *Wrong:* "There's no need for that tone."
- *Right:* Speak to them privately: "Sounds like this landed badly. What part bothers you most?" Then offer a say: "Which two fields would you want added?"

**2. The silent new joiner.** In week three, a new content associate skips team chai and answers only in one-word replies.
- *Hidden domain:* **Relatedness** (and Status: unsure their Urdu or English is "good enough" here).
- *Wrong:* Note it as "not a culture fit" or "low engagement".
- *Right:* Invite them for chai one-to-one, connect them with a peer who joined last year, and ask, "What's been hardest so far?"

**3. The angry leave email.** An analyst's Eid leave is rejected, while a colleague's overlapping request was approved. The analyst sends an all-caps email to HR.
- *Hidden domain:* **Fairness** (and Status).
- *Wrong:* Reply only about email etiquette, or quote the policy back at them.
- *Right:* "I can see why that felt unfair. Let me show you how requests were decided." Explain the criteria, and give them a real route to appeal.
